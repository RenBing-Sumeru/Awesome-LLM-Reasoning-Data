Under the same stated compute budget, the selective policy raises the mean visual-insight score from 61.64 for the baseline to 65.86 and reduces variance by 39%. The paper reports that the selected local judge has Kendall’s tau 0.55 against human experts. The result matters because it measures quality in an end-to-end workflow where no exact terminal checker can decide whether an insight is useful.

The ablations support the allocation mechanism rather than arbitrary pruning. Random pruning lowers quality as its pruning ratio rises despite similar or greater computation, while pruning only at the final insight stage produces much smaller gains than full stage-wise selection. The human analysis also finds that high-scoring reports become more consistently strong, though agreement declines among already high-quality reports because their differences are subtle.
