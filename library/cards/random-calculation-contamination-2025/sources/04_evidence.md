The strongest evidence for the data artifact is direct: the author repository publishes both the generator and generated RandomCalculation JSON files, while the official AAAI paper specifies the construction and experimental role. Inspection of commit `074a6e7e41bccbc029f7f4a4ae4b458034aa4b5b` found 21 result files: a 0-operation base file and twenty 1-20-operation files, all with 1,000 `id`/`prompt`/`answer` rows and no duplicate prompts within a file. This supports the existence of an open procedural prompt-answer release. It does not establish exact regeneration because the release lacks a fixed seed, explicit deduplication, and an immutable version tag.

The paper's partial-prompt probe reports a marked benchmark contrast for Qwen2.5-Math-7B. Given the first 60% of a prompt, exact suffix reconstruction is 54.60% and answer accuracy is 53.6% on MATH-500, versus 0% and 2.0% on LiveMathBench v202505. For Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, the corresponding figures are 3.8% and 2.4% on MATH-500, and 0% and 1.0% on LiveMathBench. These results are consistent with model- and benchmark-specific exposure, but they do not recover a pretraining corpus or prove item provenance.

On RandomCalculation, correct rewards produce sustained improvement for Qwen2.5-Math-7B, whereas random and inverted rewards are unstable or collapse. The 5-operation setting can exceed the paper's Max@16 reference; gains are smaller at 10 operations. Llama3.1-8B-Instruct does not exceed Max@16 even with correct rewards. The paper also reports greater pre/post-RL response similarity and lower KL divergence on familiar MATH items than on RandomCalculation, which is suggestive of retrieval or constrained adaptation.

These are results about a particular reward-training experiment, not proofs that RandomCalculation is intrinsically high-quality. The paper does not report human error analysis, derivation validation, structural collision testing, or a calibrated mapping from operation count to reasoning difficulty.
