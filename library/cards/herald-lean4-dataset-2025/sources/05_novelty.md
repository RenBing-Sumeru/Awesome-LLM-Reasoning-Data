Herald’s contribution is not simply the release of a large pair count. Its distinctive construction combines three sources of formal-library structure: dependency order for statement translation, retrieval from a manually annotated NL-FL bank, and tactic-state decomposition for new local propositions. This treats Mathlib4 as a structured dependency graph and interactive proof substrate rather than a flat collection of theorem strings.

The dependency-aware procedure is particularly relevant to project-scale informalization. A theorem’s natural-language meaning can depend on local definitions and prior declarations whose names alone are opaque. Translating prerequisites first lets later prompts include their already generated descriptions. Retrieval from 1,000 manual examples supplies a domain-appropriate precedent, while expert-refined prompt principles address recurrent mathematical-language errors.

The tactic-state branch converts intermediate proof obligations into extra statement pairs. This is a concrete route from proof execution state to supervised statement data: hypotheses and the current goal define a smaller formal object, and Lean can at least check that object’s formal validity. The second augmentation branch deliberately changes natural-language surface form through equivalence rewrites, abstraction, implicit-condition omission, and multilingual rendering. Together they target both data scarcity and the rigid style of literal formal-to-natural translation.

The proof release also preserves multiple views of a single tactic proof: formal and informal theorem statements, a complete formal proof, a whole informal proof, a commented proof, and context header. That makes it more useful for analyzing proof informalization than a statement-only corpus, even though the construction excludes term proofs and omits row-level validation evidence.

For this track, the important novelty is the relationship between recipe and release. Herald exposes exact train files, schemas, row counts, model weights, evaluator code, and a pinned Lean test environment, making it more inspectable than a paper-only corpus. Yet the released repository covers the inference/evaluation tail rather than the construction pipeline. The case therefore illustrates a useful distinction: open data and evaluator code do not by themselves make an open construction recipe reproducible.
