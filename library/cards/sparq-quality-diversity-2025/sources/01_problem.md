The verified source is arXiv:2506.06499v2, a 2025 preprint marked “Under review.” Synthetic reasoning pipelines often expand fixed problem sets with more solutions or ask a stronger teacher to invent new questions. The first approach cannot enlarge the problem distribution; the second lacks a natural ground-truth signal for detecting trivial, impossible, inconsistent, or repetitive generated questions.

SPARQ treats new-problem construction as an open-ended search over problem-solution pairs. It starts from 7,500 MATH training examples, recursively mutates sampled parents, estimates each child’s policy-relative difficulty with student rollouts, and represents coverage through model-assigned skill sets. The relevant data object is not merely a generated question. It includes the parent and child problem-solution pair, intended numerical answer, 16 student attempts, extracted-answer agreement, solve rate, quality score, skill cell, archive/working-set decision, and successful student solution selected as an SFT target.

The decision boundary is intentionally narrow. Numerical agreement with a generator-written intended answer is a scalable filter, not an independent proof that the problem is well posed or that its intermediate reasoning is valid. The paper itself finds that harder retained items are more likely invalid. This makes SPARQ valuable to the data-construction track as both a concrete recipe and a case study in proxy failure; no official code or generated corpus was confirmed.
