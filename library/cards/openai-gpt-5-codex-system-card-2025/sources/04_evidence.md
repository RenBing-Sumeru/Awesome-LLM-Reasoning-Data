The seven-page addendum provides model-specific safety numbers under named evaluation conditions. Table 3 on page 4 reports a malware golden-set refusal score of 1.00 for GPT-5-Codex versus 0.98 for codex-1; the set was curated by internal policy experts, but item count, uncertainty, rubric, and independence from training are not disclosed. Table 4 on page 4 reports successful ignoring of coding prompt injections at 0.98 for both GPT-5-Codex and codex-1; attacks include terminating the current task or planting an instruction for a later step. The paper does not disclose evaluation count or train/evaluation membership.

Production Benchmarks and StrongReject are separate safety evaluations. Table 1 on page 2 reports category-specific Production Benchmark scores; GPT-5-Codex ranges from 0.719 on harassment/threatening to 0.958 on sexual/exploitative and self-harm/intent, under conversations described as representative of production data. Table 2 on page 3 reports StrongReject robustness between 0.992 and 0.997 across four prompt groups. These numbers are author-reported safety scores, not evidence about the hidden SWE reward or corpus.

The launch page adds conditioned engineering evidence. SWE-bench Verified coverage changed from 477 runnable tasks at the GPT-5 launch to all 500 after an infrastructure fix. The refactor suite uses established Python, Go, and OCaml repositories; one Gitea example changes 232 files and 3,541 lines. During testing, some large tasks ran for more than seven hours while iterating over implementation and test failures. These are evaluation and product conditions, not training rollout counts or a universal terminal rule.

OpenAI employee-traffic telemetry reports that the bottom 10% of turns by model-generated tokens used 93.7% fewer tokens than GPT-5, while the top 10% spent about twice as long reasoning, editing, testing, and iterating; token counts include non-user-visible reasoning and final output. The sampling period, task distribution, raw traces, and statistical uncertainty are unavailable, so this is observational evidence rather than a controlled data or training claim.

For code review, the official launch page says evaluation uses recent commits from popular open-source repositories and experienced software engineers rate review comments for correctness and importance. It reports that GPT-5-Codex comments are less likely to be incorrect or unimportant, but gives no sample count, baseline values, repository and commit list, rubric, agreement, uncertainty, or raw labels. Hidden unit tests are not named for this review evaluation; those belong to later GPT-5.1/5.2-Codex internal PR evaluations.

All results above are direct OpenAI reports. The Card's interpretation is bounded: the data show that named evaluation surfaces exist under the stated conditions, but do not establish that their judges, tests, prompts, or metrics supplied the training reward or were independent of the task corpus.
