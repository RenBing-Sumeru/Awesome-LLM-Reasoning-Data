QED-Nano’s novelty for this atlas is the explicit coupling of rollout statistics, online outcome-reward RL, textual state compression, and test-time scaling in one open small-model pipeline. The individual ingredients are not wholly new: proof distillation, group-based RL, rubric judging, iterative refinement, and inference-time sampling all have precedents. The useful contribution is that the paper and official artifacts expose enough settings to distinguish their roles and budgets rather than collapsing them into one “more reasoning” intervention.

FineProofs-RL occupies an unusual middle ground between a prompt release and a trace release. It preserves one problem-specific rubric plus the entire scalar score population used to characterize difficulty. This supports distributional questions that a single pass rate would hide. At the same time, absence of the corresponding proofs means it offers substantially less evidence than a candidate-level rollout corpus. The score arrays reveal how many outcomes occurred and their values, but not what behavior earned them.

The rubric contract is also more structured than ordinary answer matching. Gemini 3 Pro supplies problem-specific checkpoints, zero-credit conditions, and deductions, and GPT-OSS-20B applies them to a full natural-language proof. Yet this should not be confused with process supervision: no intermediate proof step is independently checked or released with a label. A 0-7 outcome score may reflect several rubric dimensions without providing step-localized supervision.

Reasoning Cache contributes a state representation for long proof search. Instead of restarting every long attempt from the problem alone, it summarizes partial progress and conditions the next attempt on that summary. During training the paper uses a frozen Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 summarizer and three cycles; at test time the same QED-Nano family can be used within iterative scaffolds. This connects training-time data organization to inference-time computation, but it does not demonstrate that the summary is a lossless proof state.

The release is unusually informative about a negative boundary. FineProofs-SFT contains proof text, while FineProofs-RL does not. The RL dataset card discusses DeepSeek-Math-V2 generation and SFT as pipeline context, but its actual parquet schema contains only prompts, rubrics, score arrays, and statistics. Reading the prose without inspecting the schema could incorrectly turn a statistical reward artifact into a claimed raw-trace release.

The work should therefore be compared on disclosure and experimental contract, not through promotional rankings. Its reported proof scores support the feasibility of the recipe under the stated compute and judges. They do not prove that the released prompts are representative, that the rubric judge is calibrated, that score arrays are sufficient for reuse, or that larger test-time budgets improve the underlying data.
