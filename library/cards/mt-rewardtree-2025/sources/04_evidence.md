On MT-PRMBench, Table 1 reports that MT-PRM-Qwen-2.5-3B obtains 0.660 token-level accuracy and 0.863 average prefixed sequence-level accuracy; the LLaMA-based PRM obtains 0.578 and 0.776. The benchmark contract is pairwise ranking accuracy: a reward model is correct when it scores the construction-selected `chosen` item above `rejected`. These results demonstrate behavior under MT-PRMBench’s COMETKiwi-derived labels, not agreement with human token judgments.

Table 3 isolates the pair-construction choice for the Qwen backbone. On token-level MT-PRMBench, DPO trained on token-level pairs scores 0.660 versus 0.574 for vanilla pairs; KTO scores 0.644 versus 0.562. Table 4 compares supervision forms and reports 0.66 average accuracy for preference supervision versus 0.52 for direct MCTS-value regression. The authors associate the weaker value result with noisy Monte Carlo estimates and values concentrated around 0.7–0.8. This is evidence that pairwise ordering worked better in the reported setting, not that the underlying metric values are unbiased.

For the test-time application, §5.3 and Figure 3 use Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct on 500 WMT23 cases in ZH–EN and EN–RU. The paper reports that LLaMA-PRM- and Qwen-PRM-guided decoding improve EN–RU XCOMET-XL over greedy decoding by 17.5% and 17.9%, respectively. The scope is a high-resource, automatic-metric evaluation; no human evaluation or low-resource validation is reported.

Artifact evidence is concrete but incomplete: official viewers show 8,652 rows in each training variant and 1,200 rows in each benchmark variant, while the model collection exposes two 3B PRM checkpoints. These counts establish release availability. They do not establish data quality, split independence, source-rights compatibility, calibration, or reproducibility of the unreleased search decisions.
