The evidence is downstream training performance under the authors' experimental setup, not direct validation that HES labels semantic correctness or that the selected records form a high-quality reusable dataset. In the main Math SFT table, the average score is 31.83 for the full set, 31.34 for the highest-HES 20%, 34.61 for the highest-HES 80%, and 11.75 for the lowest-HES 20%. The cross-domain table is not uniform: on GPQA-Diamond, highest-HES 20% reaches 51.96 versus 47.66 for the full set, whereas on LiveCodeBench it reaches 54.43 versus 58.76; highest-HES 80% reaches 61.65 on LiveCodeBench.

RFT gains are positive on the reported averages but modest and configuration-dependent. For per-query k = 2, 4, and 8, highest-HES averages 30.10, 30.20, and 30.13, compared with random at 28.92, 28.64, and 29.33. Global selection sometimes narrows prompt coverage, and per-benchmark results are not uniformly higher. The paper's interpretation that per-query selection benefits from coverage is plausible, but no released manifest permits a record-level diversity audit.

In RL, Table 7 reports average scores of 18.34 for the full batch, 17.51 for random positives/random negatives, 19.26 for high-HES positives/random negatives, and 17.28 for high-HES positives/low-HES negatives. This supports the narrower claim that both positive selection and the negative-sampling distribution matter in this setup; it does not show that low-HES failures are universally less useful or that HES alone specifies the training reward.

Supplementary experiments repeat the comparison on OpenR1-Math-220k, Open-Math-Reasoning, another Qwen size, and a Llama-based RFT model. The 0.6B proxy-selected top 20% gives 30.20 versus 31.34 for 8B self-selection in the main Math average. Length-stratified results also show an important boundary: the highest-versus-lowest HES gap is large for short and medium traces but only 25.19 versus 24.91 for long traces. Figures 1 and 6 report higher mean HES for incorrect than correct trajectories, reinforcing that HES tracks cumulative uncertainty/complexity rather than correctness.

The tables report Average@16 for most reasoning evaluation and Average@10 for LiveCodeBench, but no confidence intervals, independent training seeds, or released run logs are provided. Benchmark gains therefore evaluate the selector under one disclosed recipe; they are not proof of dataset quality, verifier reliability, contamination safety, or release completeness.
