Prior data-selection baselines in the paper include random sampling, response length, heuristic difficulty, average token entropy, total entropy, and average entropy over high-entropy tokens. The related-work discussion also contrasts HES with gradient/influence-based selection and selectors that need a trained auxiliary model or a powerful LLM judge. Against these baselines, the specific change is to rank token positions within each response, retain only a sparse high-entropy tail, and sum rather than average its entropy.

The main direction signal is cross-stage reuse of that same statistic. Offline SFT treats HES as a demonstration-ranking feature; correctness-filtered RFT uses it after a programmatic answer outcome; online GRPO uses it to compose successful and failed rollout subsets. The RL design additionally separates the positive and negative policies instead of applying one symmetric rank rule to both pools.

What is not new is equally important: the work does not introduce token entropy, data pruning, rejection sampling, answer verification, GRPO, the upstream datasets, or a new open corpus. It also does not establish that high entropy is intrinsically good. Its own analysis associates higher HES with incorrect responses before correctness filtering, so the reusable contribution is a model-conditioned complexity/selection feature plus stage-specific gates, not a universal quality label.

For reuse, a faithful implementation must test the relative percentile, absolute threshold, and AvgHE variants; retain prompt-coverage statistics; and compare random, length, difficulty, and full-data controls under matched training and evaluation budgets. Without those checks, an apparent gain could come from correctness filtering, prompt coverage, batch composition, model calibration, or training-budget changes rather than HES itself.
