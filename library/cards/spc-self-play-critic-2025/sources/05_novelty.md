The distinctive contribution is the combination of three mechanisms around a step critic. First, the adversary edits one known-correct step rather than generating an unconstrained whole solution. This produces local counterfactual pairs sharing the same problem and prefix. Second, candidate errors are screened by their effect on downstream solver success, providing a programmatic anchor before a learned critic is asked to judge them. Third, opponent selection is adjusted across rounds so the critic continues receiving examples near its competence frontier.

This differs from a conventional process reward model trained only on static correctness annotations: SPC produces natural-language critiques and refreshes its negative examples as the generator improves. It also differs from solution-level prover–verifier self-play because the game and feedback are localized to a single reasoning step. Finally, it differs from ordinary best-of-N ranking at test time: the critic can reject and regenerate a step before the rest of a solution is produced.

The novelty should not be overstated. The initial roles still depend on PRM800K human labels and proprietary teachers, and the method uses a fixed five-type error taxonomy. The claimed elimination of manual step annotation applies to the iterative self-play stage, not to the entire lineage. The main evidence is empirical on math tasks rather than a guarantee that the game converges or that its rewards identify all meaningful reasoning errors.
