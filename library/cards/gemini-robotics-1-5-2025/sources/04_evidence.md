The general VLA evaluation covers 230 tasks and reports task-specific progress means with standard errors, with success-rate views in the appendix. Generalization is organized along visual, instruction, action, and task axes. The same general-evaluation checkpoint controls ALOHA, Franka, and Apollo without embodiment-specific post-training; earlier embodiment-specific checkpoints used less data, so their comparisons are explicitly not apples-to-apples.

Cross-embodiment appendices disclose 10 Franka-to-ALOHA tasks and 11 ALOHA/humanoid-to-Franka tasks. Motion Transfer ablations support the qualitative claim that multi-robot data helps and the MT recipe amplifies transfer, while the report notes weaker benefits for the humanoid, possibly because of the larger embodiment gap. Raw trials and item-level splits are not released.

ER 1.5 is evaluated on 15 academic embodied-reasoning benchmarks spanning pointing, image QA, video QA, progress estimation, and success detection. Text VQA answers are graded by Gemini 2.5 Flash, creating a model-judge dependency. The paper reports that embodied-reasoning performance generally improves as the thinking budget grows and each plotted point averages three runs. Exact token-budget values found only in commented TeX are not treated as published evidence here.

The full physical-agent evaluation contains eight long-horizon tasks across ALOHA and bi-arm Franka. Thinking VLA alone reaches reported progress up to 44%, while the full agent frequently approaches 80%, under task-specific point rubrics. These platform results do not share one training state: ALOHA uses the pre-training checkpoint, whereas Franka receives additional post-training for long-horizon success.

Failure analysis labels planning, success-detection, and action failures. The Flash orchestrator reports 25.5%, 6%, and 13%, respectively, or 44.5% total; the ER 1.5 orchestrator reports 9%, 4%, and 9%, or 22% total. These are author-reported subtask-level summaries without released denominators or raw episodes.

MuJoCo was aligned to real scenes and used for more than 90% of development evaluation episodes. The report presents simulation as an iteration accelerator and still requires real-robot evaluation. This percentage does not describe the training corpus, nor does simulation rank consistency establish the real-world failure distribution.
