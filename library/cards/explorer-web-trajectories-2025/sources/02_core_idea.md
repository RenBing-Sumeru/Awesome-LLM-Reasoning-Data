Explorer's contribution is a bottom-up four-role synthesis loop that turns live interaction into both a grounded task and a successful demonstration. A proposer reads the homepage screenshot and accessibility tree, creates an abstract task, and executes the first action. A refiner alternates between choosing the next action and updating the task so it remains consistent with the accumulated history. A summarizer converts the complete action/screenshot history into a high-level intent that omits procedural detail. A verifier then accepts or rejects the episode.

The effective training object is not just the final task–action pair. It is an ordered state-action episode with page observations, two action representations, evolving intent, final summary, and retention status. The action vocabulary in Appendix D comprises click, type, select, scroll up/down, and stop. The paper reports 53K unique initial proposals expanding to 94K final task descriptions, 81K of which are unique; those are description-level counts, not independent correctness guarantees. (Paper Table 9 and Appendix D.)

The feedback contract is learned judgment over environmental evidence. The GPT-4o task verifier receives the final task description, action history, all screenshots, and Markdown for the last page, and predicts success or failure. It can inspect visible trajectory evidence but cannot directly observe hidden server state, independently prove that a transaction or content change occurred, or locate the first erroneous step. Its output filters full episodes; it is not a scalar reward or step label.

Relative to static self-instruct task proposal, Synatra's indirect-knowledge demonstrations, AgentTrek's tutorial-guided replay, and concurrent exploration in a WebArena sandbox, the distinguishing change is to revise the task while executing against many live sites and then summarize what was actually done. Playwright, set-of-mark inputs, GPT-4o roles, SFT, and LLM-as-a-judge are inherited components. The same-model generation-and-verification loop is therefore both the mechanism and a correlated-error risk. (Paper §§2–3 and Appendix F.)
