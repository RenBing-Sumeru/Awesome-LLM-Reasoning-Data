HAL's central contribution is to place heterogeneous agent benchmarks behind one orchestration, logging, evaluation, cost-accounting, and publication surface while retaining benchmark-specific task and scoring semantics. The common surface is deliberately thin: a benchmark supplies tasks and an evaluator; an agent maps an input to a submission through `run(input, **kwargs) -> dict`; local, Docker, or per-task Azure VM backends execute the work; Weave captures model/tool calls; the adapter applies the benchmark's evaluator; and the leaderboard aggregates accuracy or task score together with tokens, dollar cost, and latency.

The released data object is therefore a hierarchy rather than one training record schema. At task/call level, Weave logs can preserve operation names, trace and parent-call identifiers, task linkage, timestamps, model/tool inputs and outputs, exceptions, attributes, and usage. At episode level, the benchmark adapter supplies the final submission, success/failure or scalar/subtask score, and environment-specific observations. At run level, `_UPLOAD.json` bundles observed in the official release contain `config`, `results`, `raw_eval_results`, `raw_logging_results`, `total_usage`, `total_cost`, and `git_info`. The paper and inspected bundles do not establish that every archive has exactly the same fields.

The feedback contract is **mixed and benchmark-specific**. Gold-answer comparison, unit tests, environment-state predicates, or benchmark-specific scoring judge the final task outcome. HAL then reports effectiveness, cost, and latency. A separate audit path removes redundant Weave calls, sends cleaned transcripts and benchmark metrics to Docent, expresses behavioral questions as natural-language decision-tree rubrics, and uses GPT-5 Medium as an LLM judge. Those rubric flags cover instruction following, tool-use failure, environmental barriers, self-correction, verification, and cheating/gaming. They are post-hoc annotations, not a single HAL-wide reward or demonstrated training target. The feedback can observe logged actions, outputs, evaluator results, and rubric-visible behavior; it cannot guarantee that uninstrumented calls are present, that benchmark success reflects intended reasoning, or that a flagged failure is causally responsible for an unsuccessful episode.

The closest comparison is not another unified dataset but the nine underlying benchmark stacks—Online Mind2Web, AssistantBench, GAIA, CORE-Bench Hard, ScienceAgentBench, SciCode, SWE-bench Verified Mini, USACO, and TAU-bench Airline—plus their task-specific scaffolds and evaluators. HAL does not replace their tasks or claim authorship of a common prompt corpus. Its change is the cross-benchmark execution and provenance layer: one interface, multiple backends, common call logging, cost/latency accounting, run packaging, and a shared behavioral-audit path. The component benchmarks remain the source of task meaning and terminal correctness.

For reasoning-data research, the direction signal is that an agent benchmark can expose more than a final score: task-linked action/call traces, environment and evaluator responses, errors, success/failure, costs, and run lineage can be made auditable together. The boundary is equally important: integration and trace publication do not by themselves create a frozen, licensed, privacy-reviewed, replayable, or training-ready corpus.
