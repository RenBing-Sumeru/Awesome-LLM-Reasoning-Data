The [official NAACL paper](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.552.pdf) is the primary evidence. Pages 11065–11066 describe prompt synthesis, semantic deduplication, the programmatic score, and RS pair eligibility. Pages 11066–11067 define token-level MCTS selection, expansion, blended search reward, backpropagation, verifier-only final pair extraction, and the main RS/MCTS budgets. Pages 11073–11075 give the prompt counts, MCTS self-evaluation details, and pair-yield analysis. The reported comparison supports modest, not universal, MCTS gains. For training k=4 with `(c=4,r=2)`, Table 3 reports hard scores for RS versus MCTS of 78.86 versus 79.68 on IFEval, 38.56 versus 39.22 on the k=4 synthetic evaluation, 21.66 versus 22.43 on k=5, and 14.71 versus 15.75 on k=6. Other rows include ties or RS wins on individual evaluations. The paper's defensible conclusion is that shared-prefix MCTS pairs provide marginal, relatively consistent gains and greater stability across configurations—not that MCTS dominates RS.

Pair contrast and prompt difficulty are separate findings. Larger chosen/rejected score margins generally help more than absolute scores, while mixing high- and low-contrast pairs sometimes helps but produces mixed results. With fixed training-data size and margin, moderate k=4 or k=5 prompts generally outperform k=6 prompts. Appendix Figure 6 varies RS N over {4,8,16,32,64} and reports improvement up to about N=32 followed by saturation or deterioration.

SFT/DPO comparisons support the utility of rejected responses in this controlled setting. For one RS k=4 mixed configuration, Table 9 reports IFEval hard scores of 74.77 for SFT and 78.70 for DPO; the corresponding MCTS values in Table 10 are 73.30 and 79.97. These benchmark outcomes do not prove that the verifier measures semantic response quality, that the pairs are optimal, or that the unreleased data can be reproduced.
