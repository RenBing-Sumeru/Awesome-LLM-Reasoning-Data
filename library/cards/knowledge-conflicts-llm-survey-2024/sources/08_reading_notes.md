When turning the survey into an evaluation plan, keep the construction visible. State whether context is intended to override learned knowledge, whether contextual statements conflict with one another, and whether the response should choose, explain, defer, or express uncertainty. Change one condition at a time. This reveals whether a result concerns source selection, conflict recognition, or answer form. It also avoids rewarding a system merely for repeating the latest statement when the task requires comparison of evidence. The categories are most useful as a disciplined separation of cases, not as a broad name for every incorrect answer.

Read the three categories first, then follow the discussion of causes, behaviors, and solutions. For each cited method, record what knowledge source is varied and what response counts as correct; this prevents a robustness claim from becoming too broad.

For a deeper reading, construct a small conflict record for every example. Write down the contextual statement, the model knowledge that may disagree with it, whether competing context sources also disagree, and the authority rule used to define a correct response. This record distinguishes context-memory conflict from inter-context and intra-memory conflict before a method is judged. It also reveals whether a reported improvement changes the model's use of supplied information, changes the source that is treated as authoritative, or changes only the evaluation setting. Do not transfer a result until the target system has comparable sources and a comparably stated authority rule. The survey is valuable because it makes this hidden structure legible.
