Numerical-reasoning datasets already provide natural-language steps, formal theorem-proving datasets already offer rigorous verification, scientific-paper corpora already contain equations, and multi-agent pipelines already divide complex generation tasks. STORM-BORN does not originate those components separately.

Its first concrete shift is the source object: complete derivations from contemporary technical documents rather than short competition problems or proof-assistant statements. Questions are built around formula dependencies, approximation choices, lemmas, and source context. The goal is to preserve the kind of informal but technically dense reasoning that appears in papers and appendices, including heuristic transitions that are usually omitted from answer-only datasets.

The second shift is the six-stage modularization of grounding. Formula recognition, question drafting, source answer retrieval, context collection, self-contained rewriting, and answer-type filtering are distinct operations. That separation makes each failure class conceptually inspectable—formula omission, missing prerequisite, unsupported answer, lost context, underspecified question, or non-derivational content—even though the release does not retain the intermediate records needed to audit them row by row.

The third shift is human expert selection at a deliberately small scale. The authors reduce 2,000 generated pairs to 100 based on complexity, clarity, correctness, and reasoning density, and compare top-100 with larger subsets. The intended lesson is not simply “more synthetic data”; it is that source retrieval plus expensive domain judgment can produce a small training and evaluation object. The ablation is suggestive, but it does not prove that 100 is generally optimal or identify which expert action caused each gain.

The open release adds derived train/test and multiple-choice views, enabling SFT and lower-cost option evaluation. Yet the verified novelty is the final 100-row dataset and documented recipe—not a fully transparent human-feedback corpus. Missing candidate pools, evidence spans, expert edits, and option lineage prevent direct study of the selection process that the paper presents as central.

A stronger successor would publish document versions and rights, formula/evidence spans, every agent input/output, accept/reject/revise decisions, annotator assignments and agreement, larger ablation subsets, source-disjoint splits, and calibrated independent evaluation. Those additions would convert the human-in-the-loop claim from a paper-level narrative into a reusable data contract.
