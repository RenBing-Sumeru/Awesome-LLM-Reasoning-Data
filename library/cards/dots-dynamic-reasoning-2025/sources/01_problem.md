Reasoning systems commonly apply one fixed strategy—such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), decomposition, or self-refinement—to every question. DOTS starts from the narrower observation that the useful sequence of reasoning actions depends both on the problem and on the task-solving model. A solver that benefits from programming on one MATH item may do better with CoT on another, and two solvers may prefer different paths for the same item. The data problem is therefore not only how to generate a correct trace, but how to obtain a solver-conditioned label for which reasoning-action trajectory should be used.

DOTS turns that choice into search-generated supervision. It composes a three-layer action space—analysis, solution, and verification—into 12 candidate trajectories, executes each candidate repeatedly with a designated solver, scores final answers against task ground truth, and prunes toward high-success, shorter paths. The selected trajectory, together with a GPT-4o explanation and, for the internalized variant, the solver reasoning process and answer, becomes a supervised fine-tuning target for a planner.

The auditable online object is a collection of successful and failed solver dialogues attached to action-trajectory IDs, extracted answers, and outcome scores. The final paper-side training object is narrower: a selected optimal trajectory and explanation, optionally joined with the selected solver trace. This distinction matters because the public Hugging Face release is one 4.8 GB raw MATH JSON file that exposes sampled trials—including failures—but does not document the exact raw-record count, stable splits, selected/pruned-set history, or final external- and internalized-planner SFT files.

DOTS belongs in **Rollout, Search, and Test-Time Trace Data** because repeated rollout generation, outcome-based trajectory selection, pruning budget, and solver-specific routing jointly determine the supervision. It is not evidence that a fixed action path is universally best, and its reported benchmark results do not by themselves establish release completeness, data quality, or transfer to other solvers.
