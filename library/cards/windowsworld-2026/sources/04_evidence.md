The scale claim is directly checkable. Paper Table 1 reports 181 tasks, 17 applications, 77.9% multi-app coverage, and 4.97 intermediate checks per task. Pinned `benchmark.json` contains exactly 181 rows split 39/80/50/12 across L1/L2/L3/L4 and 899 `intermediate_checks` strings, so 899/181 = 4.9669. Its one-to-five-app distribution is 40/43/86/10/2 tasks, consistent with the paper’s 77.9% multi-app figure. This verifies task-definition scale, not executed-trajectory scale.

Paper Table 2 evaluates proprietary multimodal models and GUI agents under screenshot, screenshot+accessibility-tree, Set-of-Marks, or agent settings, with fixed 15/25/40/20 step caps by level. The best aggregate result is Gemini-3-flash-preview with screenshot+A11y: **S_int 50.32%** and **S_final 20.44%**. Under the same modality, its L3 scores are 38.63% intermediate and 14.00% final. These author-reported values show a progress/completion gap; they do not imply that half the tasks are completed.

The paper tests whether cross-application difficulty is only longer horizon. On a step-matched L1/L2 subset with 10.92 versus 11.26 average minimum expert actions, `S_int` falls from **65.74% to 35.14%** and `S_final` from **46.15% to 14.29%** (Paper §4.3). The comparison supports context-switching and cross-application state maintenance as bottlenecks under that subset, although exact subset IDs are not released.

Judge validation uses 100 stratified feasible-task trajectories—24 L1, 50 L2, 26 L3—with 518 checkpoints and two human annotators. Appendix Table 7 reports Pearson correlation **0.9108** for checkpoint score and **0.8316** for final score; Cohen’s κ is **0.8668** for checkpoint decisions and **0.8271** for final judgments. The paper also reports 95% confidence intervals. This is strong sample-level agreement, not proof of error-free labels across 181 tasks, models, UI states, or later Qwen service revisions.

Negative evidence is central. Table 4 locates first failures early in feasible multi-app workflows, while Table 3 shows failed trajectories becoming less efficient as complexity rises. The appendix attributes judge disagreements to occluded or transient visual states. L4 results also expose a reward-design weakness: UiPath can score relatively well by frequently declaring incompletion, and released code accepts empty/no-action or generic FAIL without reason-sensitive verification.
