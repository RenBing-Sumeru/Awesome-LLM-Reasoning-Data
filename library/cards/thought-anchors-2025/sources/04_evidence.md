The paper’s clearest causal example is MATH problem 4682, which asks for the number of binary digits in hexadecimal \(66666_{16}\). Expected accuracy falls across sentences 6–12, then rises sharply when sentence 13 proposes converting the hexadecimal number to decimal. The downstream trace reaches the correct 19-bit answer, while the forced-answer curve does not identify the same pivotal planning move. This is a case study, not an estimate of population-wide reliability, but it illustrates the distinction between information already available and a sentence that redirects later computation.

Across the 40 main Qwen traces, Plan Generation and Uncertainty Management sentences have higher mean counterfactual importance than Fact Retrieval or Active Computation. Forced-answer importance instead emphasizes Active Computation. This comparison supports the paper’s narrower claim that the intervention definition changes which reasoning functions appear important. It does not establish that planning sentences are universally causal or beneficial across models, tasks, and decoding settings.

The white-box analyses provide convergent but qualified evidence. Receiver heads—attention heads whose downstream attention narrows toward particular sentences—show split-half reliability of \(r=.84\) in head-level kurtosis, and the 16 highest-kurtosis heads attend to similar sentences with mean pairwise correlation \(r=.56\), compared with \(r=.35\) over arbitrary heads. Plan Generation and Uncertainty Management sentences receive higher receiver-head attention than Active Computation sentences in the reported paired tests. The paper also reports that receiver-head ablation harms answer accuracy more than random-head ablation, although Appendix results show the absolute ablation comparison is not uniformly decisive at every head count.

The sentence-masking analysis offers another correspondence. Masking attention to one sentence and measuring later-token logit KL produces causal-link maps that positively correlate with a much more expensive resampling-based alternative. In the problem-4682 case study, prominent links connect initial suspicion about a leading zero, later verification, discrepancy detection, and correction. Because masking supplies out-of-distribution inputs and relies on logits as a semantic proxy, this agreement is evidence of method convergence rather than proof of an exact causal graph.

Paper v4 extends the masking analysis to correct Qwen3-30B-A3B CoTs on 2,492 MMLU questions selected from problems with non-reasoning accuracy below 50%. At the subject level, higher average accuracy is associated with stronger close-range links (\(r=.44\), \(p<.001\)) and weaker long-range links (\(r=-.54\), \(p<.001\)); mathematics and physics show the reported close/long-distance contrast. These results concern paper-side MMLU graphs, not records in the released MATH rollout corpus.

Artifact inspection confirms that the public release contains more than the paper’s derived figures. The raw tree exposes base problems, correct and incorrect base traces, sentence chunks, forced-answer and resampling continuation arrays, answer/correctness fields, function/dependency labels, and per-chunk importance statistics. `incorrect_base_solution` and `incorrect_base_solution_forced_answer` branches remain present, and individual rollout records can carry `is_correct: false`. Exact totals for incorrect continuations, malformed answers, missing answers, and failed API requests are not summarized.

Scale claims require reconciliation rather than celebration. The paper uses 20 main questions and 40 Qwen base traces; `selected_problems.json` contains 106 qualifying screening candidates; the raw HF tree has nine model-by-solution-type branches; the file-index viewer estimates 20,997 rows; and nested `solutions.json` files hold typically 10–100 continuations. These are different units. The release’s size and benchmark findings demonstrate availability and experimental utility, but neither proves uniform data quality, full coverage, clean lineage, or suitability for training.
