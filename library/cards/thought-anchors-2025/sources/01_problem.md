Reasoning-model interpretability has a granularity problem. Methods designed around a single forward pass or individual tokens do not directly explain how a long autoregressive chain of thought reorganizes itself over dozens or hundreds of sentences. A trace can contain arithmetic, planning, backtracking, fact retrieval, and self-checking, yet the final answer alone does not identify which sentence changed the subsequent trajectory. The paper therefore asks a causal question: if one sentence were absent or replaced, how would the distribution of later reasoning and final answers change?

Forced-answer probes provide only a partial answer. Interrupting a trace at a position and asking for the final answer measures how much solution information is already available at that point, but it can miss an early planning sentence whose value is expressed only through downstream steps. Thought Anchors instead treats every sentence boundary as an intervention point. It compares continuation distributions when sentence \(i\) is retained with distributions when that sentence is removed and regenerated, and separately retains forced-answer continuations as a baseline.

This turns the research question into a reasoning-data problem. The reusable object is not merely a scalar sentence score: it links a MATH prompt and a correct or incorrect base CoT to sentence chunks, arrays of counterfactual continuations, extracted final answers, correctness flags, answer-distribution statistics, function labels, and direct-dependency annotations. The release also preserves incorrect base-solution branches and incorrect sampled outcomes, which makes failure behavior inspectable rather than silently restricting the artifact to successful traces.

The main paper analysis is deliberately small in problem count but large in conditional generation. It screens 1,000 MATH problems with ten attempts each, selects 20 questions solved 25–75% of the time, and analyzes one correct and one incorrect DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B trace per question. For each analyzed sentence, the paper compares 100 continuations with the sentence kept and 100 with it removed or resampled; forced-answer variants add 100 completions at each position. This structure places the work in **Rollout / Search / Test-Time Trace Data**, although the method is an offline counterfactual rollout analysis rather than a search policy that tries to maximize correctness.

The audit problem is inseparable from the scientific one. Hugging Face reports 20,997 rows, but those rows index raw files whose `content` fields can contain arrays of continuations; they are not 20,997 individual rollouts. The paper’s experimental target of 100 samples per condition also differs from the dataset card’s statement that released chunk files typically contain 10–100 continuations. Any reuse must therefore reconcile task, branch, directory, file, chunk, and nested-continuation counts before interpreting scale or coverage.
