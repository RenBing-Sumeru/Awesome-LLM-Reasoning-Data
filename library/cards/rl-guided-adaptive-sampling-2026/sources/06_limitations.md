The controller is trained and evaluated on mathematical-answer tasks where a final answer can be counted and majority voting is meaningful; its state deliberately ignores prompt semantics, reasoning quality, hidden states, and answer confidence. The terminal reward is a surrogate based on the 32-sample running majority, not direct task correctness, so it can preserve a stable but wrong consensus. The reported cost objective is also expressed through samples and rounds, while real latency depends on model serving, batching, extraction failures, and hardware. A deployment should therefore measure wall-clock behavior, inspect wrong but highly consistent answers, and retune or retrain the policy when its answer distribution changes.
