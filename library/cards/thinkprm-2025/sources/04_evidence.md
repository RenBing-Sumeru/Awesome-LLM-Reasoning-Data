The strongest direct evidence about the released data is its filtering and measured composition. Every retained generated judgment must agree with PRM800K labels, but this check covers parsed decisions rather than all claims in the critique. The 1K set contains 486 correct and 514 incorrect prefixes from 869 unique questions; its 8,099 step labels are highly imbalanced, with 7,474 positive (92.3%) and 625 negative (7.7%). Verification chains average 1,037 tokens and range from 207 to 3,669 tokens.

ProcessBench measures verifier behavior after training, not intrinsic row quality. On OlympiadBench/OmniMath, the 1.5B base used as LLM-as-a-Judge scores 5.0/5.4 F1 with 51.4%/55.1% bad outputs, while ThinkPRM-1.5B scores 76.3/75.7 with 1.4%/2.4% bad outputs. The 14B comparison moves from 72.8/67.8 to 87.3/85.7 F1, with bad-output rates falling to 2.3% on both subsets. QwQ remains an exception: invalid-output behavior is not uniformly removed by finetuning.

A controlled data ablation supports the use of long verification traces under the tested setup. With the same 1K examples rewritten into short CoTs, 1.5B ProcessBench F1 drops from 87.3/75.7 to 64.8/66.7 and 14B drops from 87.3/85.7 to 55.3/60.8. Process-based filtering of 65K chains also outperforms outcome-based filtering of 128K chains in the reported comparison. These results connect construction choices to downstream verifier behavior, but they do not certify rationale faithfulness.

Test-time results show utility within specific candidate generators and subsets. The paper reports higher or comparable Best-of-N and guided-search curves than matched discriminative and LLM-as-a-Judge baselines, an 8-point advantage over DiscPRM on 86 GPQA-physics questions, a 4.5-point advantage on a 200-problem LiveCodeBench v5 subset, and a 7.2-point token-budget comparison on a ProcessBench subset. Parallel score averaging and sequential re-checking can help, but performance plateaus and the paper finds no universal winner between them.

Scaling the synthetic training set to 65K improves reported MATH-500 curves while reducing some out-of-domain performance. Benchmark gains therefore motivate the verifier and data recipe; they do not prove that every accepted CoT is correct, that the dataset is uncontaminated or properly licensed, or that the verifier score transfers to unseen generators, tasks, and search policies.
