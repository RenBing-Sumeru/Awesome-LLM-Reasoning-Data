**Inputs and collection.** Ninety-five crowdworkers in mainland China were instructed to invoke the collection app when they had a real current phone intent, record that intent and a location/scenario category, and demonstrate the operation on their daily-use Android phone. Each participant collected for one month with at most 12 uploads per day. The recorder stores user ID, timestamp, app/activity, screenshots, accessibility trees, and actions; a separate profile table includes sex, age, occupation, address, marital/family status, and phone brand (Paper §4.2, Figure 3, Appendix A.1/A.3/A.4; official README and CSVs).

**Interaction and output.** Each screenshot aligns with an action from `click`, `long_click`, `type`, `scroll`, `press_back`, `press_home`, `press_recent`, `wait`, and terminal `finish/finished`; the release also stores the corresponding accessibility-tree XML. An episode directory is keyed by user and timestamp and contains the intent description, context, JPG/XML observations, and JSONL action trace. The collector adds `finish` to the last screenshot, but the schema has no dense reward or independently verified per-episode success field (Paper Table 2, Appendix A.4/A.7; official repository).

**Quality and privacy filtering.** Collectors were trained to avoid ambiguous intents, redundant or useless operations, and excessively fast or repetitive actions. Timed manual checks removed low-quality episodes while retaining minor realistic mistakes. Participants signed a data-usage agreement, were warned not to upload private information, could inspect/delete uploads during collection, and collected only when actively invoking the app. Obvious privacy cases were manually removed; Qwen-VL-Max then screened first and last screenshots, and humans rechecked flagged episodes. Numeric rejection thresholds, removal counts, the privacy rubric, and the agreement text are undisclosed (Appendix A.3).

**Splits and release audit.** The paper reports train 16,000/177,674 screenshots, validation 4,411/32,859, suggestion test 1,000/10,412, and execution test 200/2,074; test examples come from each user's last 20% chronologically, while training is sampled from the first 60%, so the main split is not user-disjoint. In the official CSVs, `train_set.csv` has 16,000 unique keys; `test_suggestion.csv` has 1,000 rows but only 996 unique `(user_id,time)` keys because four rows are exact duplicates; `test_execution.csv` has 200 unique keys. The tests share 172 unique keys, corresponding to 174 suggestion-row occurrences because two duplicated suggestion keys are also in execution. No validation CSV or corpus-wide deduplication/decontamination procedure is released (Paper Appendix A.5, Table 7; official CSV audit).

**Benchmark pipeline.** Suggestion builds one prompt from profile, time, scenario, up to 20 previous intents, and 0–3 screenshots, then scores the Chinese intent sentence with `Sim1` and paper-only `SR1`. Execution retrieves the most intent-similar earlier same-user action sequence, prompts on the current screenshot/accessibility elements and action history, executes the predicted action through ADB/uiautomator2, and repeats until `finished()` or the 2.5× step limit. `SR2` still requires manual final-state review; the released result writer initializes and records `success=0` rather than performing that review.

**Training and reproducibility.** The authors separately format the two tracks for LoRA SFT of Qwen-2.5-VL-7B, using rank 4 or 64 and either 1,000 proportionally sampled episodes or all 16,000; a joint-model ablation is also reported. No RL, RLVR, preference modeling, reward modeling, or PRM training is disclosed. Loss, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, epochs, seed, training code, configurations, and checkpoints are unknown. Reproduction must pin arXiv v2, GitHub commit `e73a4dac3bd13ea32b7836525ff074a69cb047ea`, and Kaggle version 2, then separately reconstruct training and the live device/app state; the public release supplies neither a resettable environment nor a deterministic replay bundle.
