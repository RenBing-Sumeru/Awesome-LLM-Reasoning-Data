Large reasoning models generate long thinking drafts before their final answers, but it is unclear whether those steps actually influence later reasoning. Some text may be post-hoc narration, and the final answer may not depend on the draft conclusion. Logical consistency between draft and answer does not prove causal use. If the draft is readable narration rather than a real control signal, using it for safety monitoring or human auditing creates false interpretability. If a thinking draft is readable narration without controlling later generation, using it for safety monitoring creates false interpretability. Surface consistency cannot distinguish causal dependence from post-hoc rationalization.

This work introduces a counterfactual intervention framework that separately measures how individual draft steps affect later trajectories and whether the final answer depends on the draft conclusion, testing faithfulness at two levels.
