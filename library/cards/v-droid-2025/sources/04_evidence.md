ArXiv v5 reports end-to-end task success of 59.5% on AndroidWorld, 38.3% on AndroidLab, and 49.0% on MobileAgentBench. Against the cited prior-best values, the corresponding absolute margins are 5.2, 2.1, and 9.0 percentage points (v5 Abstract and §6.2/Fig. 9, pp. 9–10). AndroidWorld is treated as in domain; AndroidLab and MobileAgentBench are out of domain and their apps are excluded from collection. These are author-reported benchmark results, not an independent reproduction or a record-level audit of the training pairs.

The scale study reports AndroidWorld success of 15.0%, 37.5%, 47.4%, and 59.5% at cumulative pair counts of 9K, 27K, 55K, and 110K. MobileAgentBench moves from 17.0% to 49.0% over the same staged process (v5 §6.3, Fig. 12, p. 11). The association is consistent with a stronger verifier after later rounds, but it is not an isolated data-volume effect: app/task diversity, human-versus-agent annotation mix, correction policy, and model checkpoint also change. Pair count therefore cannot by itself certify pair quality.

For self-correction, adding the sampled reverse-action data changes AndroidWorld success from 52.2% to 59.5% and mean trajectory length from 10.3 to 11.6 steps (v5 §6.2, Fig. 11 discussion, p. 11). The longer trajectories are interpreted as improved ability to recover, but the method section also reports a direct negative result: too much self-correction data produces repeated-back collapse, motivating the approximately 2.5% cap. This supports the filtering choice while showing that a seemingly correct reverse-action reward can distort behavior.

Entropy triage is useful but not calibrated. At the median-entropy threshold, Table 1 reports correctness-classification accuracy of 0.51, 0.76, and 0.71 for the 27K, 55K, and 110K rounds; Fig. 13 reports AUC of 0.55, 0.83, and 0.78. The final-round values are lower than the preceding round, so uncertainty-based human review has both false positives and false negatives and is not a monotonic quality guarantee (v5 Table 1, p. 9; Fig. 13, pp. 11–12).

Working memory is a major experimental dependency. LLM-generated memory yields 59.5% AndroidWorld success versus 46.1% for rule-based memory and 40.0% for action history alone. The current implementation uses GPT-4 for this component, but its snapshot and settings are not disclosed (v5 §6.4 and Table 4, p. 12). Latency likewise must be versioned: v5 reports 4.3 seconds total per step, about 0.7 seconds per decision, and 0.74-second decision latency in Table 2; GPT-4 memory construction accounts for 3.03 seconds. The v1/project/README claim of 0.7 seconds per step is stale, as is their 9.5-point AndroidWorld margin versus the v5 margin of 5.2 points.

The paper's failure analysis names hallucinated decisions, inaccurate memory, incomplete rule-extracted action spaces, and inability of text-only input to understand image/video content (v5 §6.6, Fig. 14, p. 13). These results support the verifier-driven design under the reported benchmarks, but they do not demonstrate that the unreleased 110K records are correctly labeled, deduplicated, licensed, privacy-reviewed, or replayable.
