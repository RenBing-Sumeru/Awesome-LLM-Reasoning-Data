The main reported evidence is agreement with humans. The paper reports 3K controlled expert votes and 3K sampled crowdsourced votes, with GPT-4 judge agreement above 80% and comparable to human-human agreement. On MT-Bench second-turn non-tie comparisons, GPT-4 pairwise judging reaches 85% agreement with humans while human-human agreement is 82%; on Chatbot Arena non-tie comparisons, GPT-4 pairwise judging reaches 87% agreement with human votes.

The artifact evidence is unusually concrete for a judge paper: 80 MT-Bench questions, released human annotations for 6 model answer sets, FastChat `llm_judge` scripts, and a Hugging Face dataset card for `lmsys/mt_bench_human_judgments` listing 3.3K expert-level pairwise human preferences under cc-by-4.0. At row level, the decisive evidence is not a correctness label; it is whether an independent human or GPT judge preferred response A, response B, or tie under a specified prompt and ordering.

The evidence boundary is important. Agreement improves when model quality gaps are large and weakens near ties; position bias can flip judgments under answer-order swaps; math answers can be misgraded unless prompts or references constrain the judge. Leaderboard reuse must therefore preserve judge prompt, answer order, tie policy, model version, and data snapshot.
