Read each method family with two columns in mind: what resource it saves and what behavior might change. Then check whether the cited evaluation matches your target hardware and task instead of carrying over an efficiency claim without context.

Use the survey as a disciplined comparison guide. First state the deployment constraint: memory, computation, latency, or another resource. Next identify whether quantization, pruning, or knowledge distillation is the mechanism being discussed, and do not infer that their trade-offs are identical. Then place the reported saving beside the task-quality measure used in the cited study. A smaller model can be useful only relative to an intended workload and a stated quality floor. Check whether the benchmark reflects that workload, whether the hardware assumptions match, and whether the evaluation observes the behavior your users need. Keep model size, computational cost, task score, and measurement conditions in the same note. This prevents a result about one practical setting from being recast as a general claim that compression has no meaningful loss.

When comparing two reports, make the comparison conditional rather than absolute. Ask whether they start from comparable base models, use the same resource measure, and test the same task behavior. A reduction in stored parameters is not automatically a reduction in every deployment cost, and a preserved benchmark score does not by itself cover all uses. The survey's method families are useful because they force the reader to name the mechanism before interpreting the result. Record what was changed, what was measured, and what remained outside the measurement. If a paper emphasizes an efficiency number, look for the quality outcome beside it; if it emphasizes task quality, look for the resource budget beside it. This paired reading keeps the decision focused on an explicit trade-off instead of an attractive isolated result.

Finally, decide in advance which outcome would make the method unsuitable. It may be a quality drop on a required task, a resource demand beyond the available setting, or an evaluation that does not represent the intended use. This boundary turns the survey from a list of techniques into a practical selection aid. It also makes a negative result informative: a method can be valuable for one constraint and unsuitable for another without either conclusion being contradictory.

Keep the conclusion narrow: report the method, the resource saved, the quality measure retained, and the setting in which that trade-off was observed. A reader can then decide whether the same trade-off matches a different deployment instead of inheriting a conclusion with its conditions removed.

This final record is also the simplest way to compare later alternatives fairly.
