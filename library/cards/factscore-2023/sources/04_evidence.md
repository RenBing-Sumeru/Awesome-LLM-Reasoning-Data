The paper's main evidence has two parts. First, it reports human FActScore annotations for long-form people biographies, including 183 labeled entities used for the detailed human-evaluation setting. Second, it reports automatic evaluation on 6,500 generations from 13 later language models, a scale the authors estimate would have cost about $26K under human evaluation.

The row-level evidence is the atomic support label. The final percentage is only an aggregate over those labels. The ACL abstract reports that the automatic model estimates FActScore with less than 2% error relative to human evaluation, and the official README reports a 0.99 Pearson correlation between two recommended automatic estimators. Those numbers support using the package for audits, but only under the paper's domain, prompts, knowledge source, and estimator settings.

The evaluation findings also illustrate why the metric is needed: long-form responses can have many facts per answer, different response ratios, and different factual precision. A high number of generated facts is not the same as high factual precision. Evidence remains bounded by biography-style topics, the selected Wikipedia snapshot or custom source, decomposition quality, retrieval failures, and support-judge drift.
