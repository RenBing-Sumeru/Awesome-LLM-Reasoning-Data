Earlier adaptive-compute approaches often use a hand-designed difficulty score or threshold, while uniform self-consistency fixes one sample count for all questions. AdaCompute instead begins with a global constrained objective, prices each budget with one dual variable, derives input-specific labels from empirical utility, and learns to predict those labels before expensive inference. This exposes budget choice as a supervised data object rather than an implicit runtime heuristic.

The paper does not introduce self-consistency, majority voting, Lagrangian relaxation, gradient-boosted trees, or difficulty features individually. Its specific contribution is the SOLVE-THEN-LEARN composition, cost-monotonic budget targeting, and formal imitation-regret analysis for this allocation setting. The oracle's exactness is with respect to the finite empirical utility table and stated cost model; it is not ground truth about future deployment.

For Rollout, Search, and Test-Time Trace Data, the direction signal is a reusable question-budget record linking multiple responses to utility estimates and a derived allocation label. Its quality must be judged from sampling variance, answer parsing, budget accounting, split discipline, and distribution stability. Higher benchmark accuracy under matched sample budgets is evidence for the allocator, not proof of data quality.
