The paper treats the X in Chain-of-X as the key design choice and builds one taxonomy around the type of node used in a chain. A second taxonomy groups methods by application task. Together, they prevent a common confusion: two methods may both be sequential, yet differ fundamentally in what each intermediate item represents or in what end task the chain serves. For a reader, this produces a simple comparison frame. First identify the node semantics: is the chain carrying thoughts, actions, code, retrieved material, plans, or another structure? Then identify the application and final output. Only after those two questions are answered does it make sense to compare prompting, cost, transparency, or accuracy across papers. The framework also makes missing descriptions visible: a paper that cannot state its node semantics is difficult to evaluate precisely.
