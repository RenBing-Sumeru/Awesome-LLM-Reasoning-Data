The answer contract can produce false negatives. Inverted questions establish that the supplied reference is valid more readily than they establish that all valid answers are known. Trainers add constraints and revise items when another person finds a different valid answer, but the paper explicitly does not guarantee uniqueness. A single-reference LLM judge can still reject an alternative answer, and the paper does not disclose its grader checkpoint, decoding settings, validation set, calibration, or sensitivity analysis.

The current `browsecomp_eval.py` scorer is blocked by a concrete label-handling mismatch: it returns `correct: yes/no` but compares with `yes/no`. No BrowseComp unit test catches the defect, and the repository has no tagged evaluator release or dependency lock. The current runner's `gpt-4.1-2025-04-14` grader postdates the OpenAI release and cannot be assumed to match the paper grader. These facts block direct reproduction from the current reference code; they do not prove that the paper results, produced through an undisclosed evaluation path, are invalid.

The released object omits the process that the benchmark is meant to stress. There are no model responses, confidence records, queries, tool calls, page observations, URLs, citations, screenshots, timestamps, retries, success paths, failure paths, or grader transcripts from the reported experiments. The live web, search rankings, access policies, commercial products, and page contents drift, while no web snapshot, browser image, reset state, locale, timeout, or deterministic replay fixture is supplied. The answer-only judge cannot assess citation support, source quality, browsing faithfulness, persistence, or process efficiency.

The anti-contamination layer is limited. Question and answer plaintext are reversibly obfuscated, but the public canary/password and official decryptor recover them. The canary GUID and request not to repost examples may aid filtering if honored; no post-release exposure measurement, training-corpus overlap scan, refreshed or hidden holdout, or removal guarantee is reported. Public release therefore creates continuing contamination risk.

Construction and release metadata also remain incomplete. Review removed 21 of 118 Deep Research zero-pass items, conditioning the final set on an undisclosed model version while withholding removed rows and reasons. Record-level factual sources, evidence, trainer/validator identities, dates, adjudication, and upstream rights are absent. The repository is MIT-licensed and labels BrowseComp accordingly, but the separately hosted CSV has no embedded data license or data card; this does not establish rights to all underlying web facts. Trainer count, demographics, compensation, consent, PII review, ethics review, and privacy policy are unknown.

Finally, Deep Research was trained on data intended to teach BrowseComp-like task skill. That disclosure does not establish use of the released 1,266 rows, but exact training overlap is unknown. Main-evaluation retry policies and budgets are unknown, and the reported test-time-compute curve lacks units. These gaps constrain attribution across model capability, browsing scaffold, grader, data construction, and extra inference compute.
