All quantitative results below are author-reported from arXiv v4 and were not independently reproduced. In the reported construction, single-judge acceptance is 78.0% on 3,000 WebArena failures and 82.5% on 5,000 ToolBench failures; cross-model MJ-X acceptance is 71.5% and 75.5%, respectively. These yields show how selective the pipeline is under the paper's judges, not how complete or reusable the unreleased corpus is.

Table 1 reports MJ-X success of 29.9, 37.7, 26.5, and 24.8 on WebArena for GPT-4o, Qwen2.5-72B, Qwen2.5-7B, and LLaMA-3.1-8B, with gains of 7.6–8.7 points over SFT-Success. On ToolBench the corresponding scores are 75.4, 83.5, 72.6, and 69.0, with gains of 7.6–11.4 points. Under the paper's infrastructure, MJ-X is 3.0–6.2 points above AWM. These comparisons support the claim that the relabeled records can be useful in the reported training setup; they do not by themselves validate label correctness, release completeness, or data rights.

The judge ablation is more directly relevant to the data contract. On WebArena, cross-model MJ-X obtains 26.5% success with 2.9% label noise; same-model MJ-S obtains 26.0% with 4.4% noise; a single judge obtains 25.7% with 5.9% noise. Removing the confidence filter drops success to 22.3% and raises label noise to 15.0%. This supports treating judge identity and filtering as construction variables rather than incidental evaluation settings.

Human evaluation samples 200 pairs per benchmark, uses three NLP-PhD annotators and majority vote, and reports MJ-X precision of 97.1% on WebArena and 96.0% on ToolBench, with Fleiss kappa 0.82 and 0.79. The same audit finds that 38.7% of WebArena and 35.8% of ToolBench pairs rejected by the filter are valid according to humans. Precision is therefore accompanied by substantial false-negative selection bias; the study does not establish recall for the full source distribution or live-environment validity of hindsight goals.

Further evidence is conditional. Qwen2.5 models from 1.5B to 72B all improve under the reported setup, and iterative Qwen2.5-7B redeployment on WA-HELDOUT rises from 17.8 to 26.5, 27.9, and 28.2 over rounds 0–3 while acceptance declines from 71.5% to 68.4% and 66.0%. A 363-trajectory looping subset has only 24.2% recoverability, 21.5% acceptance, and a 3.4-point gain versus 9.5 points for non-looping failures. These negative and scaling results help locate where the recipe works; none turns benchmark performance into proof of data quality.
