The central claim is that test-time compute has diminishing, and sometimes negative, marginal utility. The authors make this visible by following the same question across many forced reasoning budgets. An answer changing from wrong to correct is a positive flip; changing from correct to wrong is a negative flip. When negative flips dominate, giving the model more room to think is no longer a neutral expense but a source of error.

They therefore treat stopping as a cost-sensitive allocation problem. Accuracy should be read together with a compute penalty and an efficiency frontier, not as an isolated score at the largest token limit. Difficulty matters because easy problems reach the harmful regime sooner than hard ones, so a uniform budget wastes computation and can actively damage outputs that a shorter trajectory had already solved.
