The prior self-training baseline is answer-level rejection. STaR and RFT retain rationales that lead to a correct final prediction; other preference methods exploit correct-versus-incorrect traces or finer error signals. CREST keeps the original correctness gate but adds a deterministic family of probes derived from the option set, asking whether the same rationale supports the correct yes/no decision for every choice.

The specific construction novelty is the reuse of one option-wise feedback object in two transformations. Its count filters SFT candidates with a configurable tolerance, while its strict ordering creates a second preference pool among candidates that are already correct on the original question. Mixing that pool with ordinary correct-versus-incorrect preferences separates "gets the answer right" from "supports more option decisions consistently."

This is an operational data recipe, not a new source of ground truth. The follow-up labels are determined by the original multiple-choice label, the probes are answered by the same checkpoint, and the aggregate score remains outcome-based. CREST does not introduce self-generated rationales, LoRA SFT, DPO, final-answer rejection, behavioral consistency testing or an independent rationale-faithfulness test.

For reuse, the most important comparison is not only whether `M_CREST` beats a baseline. A sound replication should hold the 16-candidate pool and training budget fixed, compare answer-only, option-vector, count-only and independently evaluated variants, report parser errors and pool yields, and retain ties and rejected candidates. Without those controls, downstream accuracy cannot identify which individual construction decision improved the data.
