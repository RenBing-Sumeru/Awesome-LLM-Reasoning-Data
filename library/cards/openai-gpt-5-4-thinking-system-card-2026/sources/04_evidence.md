Version is part of the evidence. The official Safety Hub records March 5, 2026 publication, a March 17 mini appendix, and an April 24 Chain of Thought update. The current 38-page PDF includes both later changes despite its March 5 cover; CoT claims below therefore belong to the April update and retrieval snapshot, not the launch state.

Training evidence is strongest for objective direction rather than implementation. Section 3.7 distinguishes a destructive-action evaluation from long-rollout training that teaches agents to revert their own changes while preserving implicit simulated user work. Section 3.8 states that computer-use training now follows platform policy plus configurable developer policy. Section 5.3.3 describes cyber safety training for maximally helpful dual-use support while refusing or de-escalating harmful actions. None provides training counts, detector, reward, labels, or causal ablation.

Safety evaluation construction is concrete. Dynamic mental-health, emotional-reliance, and self-harm evaluations generate varied adversarial multi-turn trajectories and check every assistant message for policy compliance (current PDF pp.4–5, Table 2). The production-like pilot uses deidentified recent GPT-5.2 Thinking conversations, resamples the final assistant turn with GPT-5.4 Thinking, and automatically labels the completion (pp.5–7). It is point-in-time, model-only, and subject to traffic, pipeline, and safety-stack drift.

The April monitorability update covers 13 evaluations across 24 environments and three archetypes, with GPT-5 Thinking as monitor and g-mean-squared plus cross-fit filtering. OpenAI reports label and grader mismatch in Anti-Scheming and Memory, dependence on short CoTs and few positive examples, and plans to drop those environments until revised. CoT-Control contains more than 13,000 benchmark problem/instruction pairs and is stratified by CoT length; low controllability is an audit result, not proof that non-user-visible reasoning is impossible.

Split evidence is local. Both connector and function-call prompt-injection evaluations are splits of training data, so they do not test unseen-attack generalization (PDF pp.7–8). Cyber safety evaluation is explicitly non-overlapping with training and combines deidentified production and synthetic rare cases selected with classifier flags and embedding clustering (Section 5.3.3, Table 15); the set is intentionally challenging and not representative of normal traffic. Named held-out or uncontaminated biology claims are also scoped and do not establish global decontamination.

Programmatic evaluation conditions remain evaluation-only. CVE-Bench uses three rollouts, 34 of 40 tasks, and pass@1; Cyber Range uses 16 trials and any-success pass/fail; external cyber runs allow 1,000 turns with compaction every 100K tokens. Monorepo-Bench requires all human-written hidden unit tests to pass. HealthBench has 5,000 conversations with example-specific rubrics, while first-person fairness uses more than 600 challenging prompts with GPT-4o ratings compared against humans. These numbers identify data and scoring contracts, not the reasoning-RL reward.

Deployment evidence is separate: the two-tier cyber monitor uses a topical classifier followed by a safety-reasoner monitor and can trigger message or actor controls and human review. The report supports existence of this safeguard stack, not its use as training feedback or a model-only evaluation score.
