Use the taxonomy as a comparison worksheet. First record the input representation: text alone, a diagram, or both. Next record the formal constraints and the proof artifact, because a natural-language explanation, a semi-formal sequence, and a machine-checked proof make different claims. Finally record the backend and what exactly it checks. This separates a useful proposed construction from a certificate accepted under stated formal rules. It also makes the weak links visible: an ambiguous diagram, a missing condition, a faulty conversion to constraints, or a candidate step that cannot be checked. When a system reports a correct final answer, ask whether that answer was independently checked, whether all steps were checked, and whether the check covers the intended geometric statement. Keep these questions separate from how readable the explanation is. The survey supports this careful comparison of heterogeneous systems; it does not supply one uniform task or score that resolves all of these distinctions.

Read the taxonomy before comparing individual systems. For each system, ask three concrete questions: how is the problem formalized, what proof artifact is produced, and which backend decides whether that artifact is accepted?

Add a fourth question: which part of the result is useful explanation and which part is formally checked evidence? A natural-language plan, a semi-formal trace, and a checked proof can all help a learner, but they make different promises. Trace the pipeline from text or diagram to constraints, candidate steps, and the backend decision. At every transition, note what information might be ambiguous or lost, especially diagram interpretation and unstated non-degeneracy conditions. When comparing systems, keep answer accuracy separate from step validity and from the existence of a checkable final artifact. This makes it possible to value flexible language-model assistance without mistaking it for a guarantee supplied by a symbolic checker.
