Earlier test-time scaling evaluations largely ask whether additional samples, branches, or thinking tokens raise an aggregate benchmark score. This work changes the decision boundary by requiring that the answer hold across languages and under comparable inference cost. It places outcome rewards, process rewards, and budget forcing on one accounting surface, then examines whether each mechanism changes not just mean accuracy but the stability of reasoning behavior across language realizations of mathematical tasks.

The reusable novelty is the negative but actionable diagnostic: a scaling technique can be effective in English while remaining linguistically brittle. MCLM and the released multilingual model make this failure measurable rather than anecdotal. The work does not introduce a universal remedy for the gap; translated supervised data improves some reported results but does not make test-time scaling language invariant. Its contribution is a stronger evaluation contract for claiming general inference-time gains.
