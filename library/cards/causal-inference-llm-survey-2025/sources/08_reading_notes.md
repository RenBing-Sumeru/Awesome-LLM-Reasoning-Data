Read the two directions separately and keep their outputs distinct: model evaluation or improvement on one side, causal discovery or effect estimation on the other. For each cited paper, record its causal question, assumptions, data, and measurement. This habit makes causal language more precise.

Add a short assumption sheet for every study. State the causal quantity or relationship under discussion, the data available, the role assigned to the language model, and what observation is used as evidence. Then ask whether the work uses causal ideas to assess or improve an LLM, or uses an LLM to assist a causal task; these directions can share vocabulary while producing different outputs. Do not turn a plausible explanation into a causal conclusion without the assumptions that justify it. Fairness, safety, and explainability may motivate causal analysis, but they do not make causal identification automatic. When comparing reported results, preserve the setting, data, and target quantity from the primary work. The survey is most useful for locating these distinctions and the relevant studies, not for supplying one shared estimator, dataset, or universal guarantee.
