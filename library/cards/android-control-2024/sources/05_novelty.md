Prior UI-control datasets in the paper’s Table 1 provide combinations of demonstrations, screen observations, and instructions; Android in the Wild also serves as a transfer baseline in the experiments. AndroidControl changes the supervised object by pairing a human task-level goal with a human instruction before every action, while covering 14,548 unique tasks across 833 apps. This supports a controlled comparison between HL inputs, which require the model to infer intermediate steps, and LL inputs, which expose the current subgoal (Paper Table 1, pp. 3–4; §4.2, pp. 6–7).

The second concrete change is the evaluation design around data scale and distribution shift. The study subsamples 5 through all available training episodes and reports IDD, app-unseen, task-unseen, and category-unseen surfaces, rather than treating one aggregate offline score as sufficient. That makes the release a direction signal for asking whether more demonstrations improve reference-action imitation uniformly or mainly densify known apps and task templates.

What is not new remains important. The work does not introduce Android, accessibility-tree control, human behavior cloning, LoRA, PaLM-2, offline next-action accuracy, or an online reward interface. Its collection pipeline uses an LLM only to seed generic feature descriptions and personas; target actions and instructions come from humans. The release is neither an RLVR dataset nor a replayable environment, and the reported scale itself is not a record-level quality signal.

For reasoning-data research, the useful novelty is the combination of instruction granularity, full episode context, and controlled distribution-shift scaling. Before reusing it, a builder still must reconcile the 13,604/13,603 training-count mismatch, determine which collection statuses are serialized, inspect the shards, resolve the license conflict, and recreate the omitted SeqIO and evaluation logic. Without those checks, the novelty supports study design but not a fully reproducible training claim.
