All numerical results below are author-reported from the ICLR 2026 paper and were not independently reproduced. The primary comparison holds the policy-update algorithm fixed and replaces stochastic rollouts with LATR for 500-step Qwen2.5-3B Base runs.

**Final accuracy.** On Countdown with DAPO, Pass@1 rises from 70.7 for stochastic rollouts to 74.7 with LATR. On the DAPO-Math validation set with DAPO, it rises from 26.8 to 32.5. The same DAPO comparison reports 37.8 to 45.3 on AMC-2023, 60.4 to 62.6 on MATH-500, and 28.1 to 30.4 on Olympiad-Bench (Tables 1–3). Across the task–optimizer combinations summarized by the authors, the reported average final Pass@1 improvement is 4.2 percentage points. These comparisons support a claim about the training recipe under the reported model, tasks, verifier, and response budget; they do not establish quality of the processed HF rows.

**Learning speed and systems cost.** The paper reports 131% average learning acceleration and approximately 2.3 times fewer steps/time to reach comparable performance, while each LATR training step is about 10% slower because dynamic branch insertion, deletion, and comparison remain sequential (Sections 4.2–4.3; Appendix C.4). “131% acceleration” must not be read as 131% lower wall-clock cost. The official repository further reports about 130 GB peak GPU memory on eight H200 GPUs and warns that reducing rollout micro-batch size trades efficiency for memory.

**Diversity and ablations.** Table 4 reports more distinct Countdown answers per group and higher Pass@8 under LATR, linking the intervention to group diversity rather than only final Pass@1. The same table gives an important negative result: for a Qwen2.5 model trained with LATR, stochastic evaluation decoding yields Pass@1 70.9, whereas LATR evaluation decoding yields 68.9. This supports the authors’ train–test-mismatch concern and argues against treating LATR as a default inference method. Table 5 shows that random branching is unstable, no or random pruning underperforms, and token-level lookahead is weaker than trajectory-level lookahead; the benefit therefore does not follow from adding arbitrary extra branches.

**Generalization and scope.** Section 5.5 and Table 6 extend experiments to Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-1.7B-Base, but the verified evidence covers only arithmetic/logical Countdown and mathematical problem solving with programmatic final-answer verification. It does not demonstrate code generation, formal proof, open-ended judging, human-feedback tasks, or non-verifiable environments.

**Artifact evidence.** The official code implements a VeRL rollout worker, reward functions, branch/prune filters, and GRPO/DAPO scripts at inspected commit `2adb0edf24cc9a9342ac021892897adcec1a2738`. The official HF repository at commit `082e3b599960ef8341d1f00be8ae474d994345cf` contains four processed Parquet files totaling 24,668,606 bytes and is tagged `100K<n<1M`; exact row counts per file were not stated in the checked metadata. Checked rows contain prompts and ground-truth reward metadata, not the search traces needed to reproduce the diversity or pruning analyses.

The strongest supported conclusion is conditional: under the paper’s Qwen/VeRL, task, verifier, and eight-response settings, structured trajectory-level branching and pruning can produce more useful on-policy reward groups and improve learning relative to stochastic sampling. Benchmark gains are not evidence that every branch is semantically diverse, that raw data are available, or that the recipe is reproducible without resolving configuration drift.
