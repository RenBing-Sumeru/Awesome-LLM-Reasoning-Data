Process reward models must decide where a reasoning trace first becomes invalid. A cheap discriminative verifier can emit a binary label for every step, but its supervision may miss subtle mathematical errors and provides little diagnostic context. A generative verifier can analyze a step before judging it, but always generating an explanation makes long-trace verification expensive.

The data problem is therefore not only to label steps, but to decide how much verification work each step warrants. The raw supervision used by Dyve is also noisy: OmegaPRM proposes error locations from search rollouts, and those proposals can be wrong. The paper addresses both issues with a filtering-and-target-synthesis pipeline that tries to preserve inexpensive labels for straightforward steps while reserving explanatory targets for difficult or erroneous ones.
