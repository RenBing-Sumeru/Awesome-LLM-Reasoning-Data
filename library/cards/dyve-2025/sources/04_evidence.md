On ProcessBench, the 14B Dyve model reports F1 scores of 68.5 on GSM8K, 58.3 on MATH, 49.0 on OlympiadBench, and 47.2 on OmniMATH. A System-2-only variant scores 70.0, 60.1, 50.1, and 49.6 respectively, so the adaptive model is not the highest-accuracy setting in this comparison. Its benefit is the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff: System 1 is used for 27.5%, 30.7%, 41.0%, and 32.6% of steps on those four domains, with reported average verification times of 0.47, 0.51, 1.25, and 2.05 seconds.

The data ablation is consistent with filtering mattering: MATH F1 rises from 34.7 for unfiltered Monte Carlo data to 56.0 after consensus filtering, then to 58.3 after stepwise flagging. Transfer is not uniformly positive, however. For Qwen2.5-14B, mixed training changes the four scores from 28.7/27.9/23.7/24.6 to 51.7/42.3/30.2/23.6, including a one-point decline on OmniMATH. Qwen3-8B and the DeepSeek-R1-14B base improve on all four reported domains.

In best-of-N selection on MATH-500, Dyve reaches 95.5 at N=8 with a DeepSeek-R1-14B proposer and 90.4 with Qwen2.5-MATH-7B-Instruct. The paper states that outcomes were manually verified. Appendix A.5 estimates the shared rollout-generation cost at 1,344 A800 GPU-hours and contrasts approximately $2,500 of DeepSeek-V3 API labeling with $585,000 under a $5-per-human-label assumption. That comparison depends on its pricing assumptions and excludes the shared rollout cost.

These results support the utility of the trained verifier and the contribution of filtering, but they do not directly measure the precision, calibration, or provenance quality of the released labels. The public release itself supplies a further audit datum: its current 156,321 rows do not match the approximately 117,000 traces reported in the paper.
