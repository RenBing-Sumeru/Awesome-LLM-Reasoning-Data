The contribution is a common thought-action-result lens for comparing heterogeneous software-engineering agent runs below the final patch. The authors convert three agents' logs into ordered interaction triples, measure structural properties, map actions into a shared taxonomy, mine repeated action sequences, and manually label how adjacent thoughts, actions, and results relate.

The data object is a full episode plus analytical views. At episode level it carries the agent and benchmark instance, an ordered sequence of thought, action/tool call, arguments, and result/observation, iteration and token totals, and a binary resolved outcome. At process level it adds one of eight action categories—Explore, Locate, Search, Reproduce, Generate Fix, Run tests, Refactor, or Explain—and labels five relation families: thought-action, thought-thought, action-action, result-thought, and result-action. Frequent-sequence analysis uses action 4-grams.

The feedback contract is deliberately mixed. During a run, tools and the repository environment return observations, but those observations are not per-step correctness labels. At episode end, AutoCodeRover and OpenHands are successful when a patch passes SWE-bench tests. RepairAgent first needs a Defects4J-plausible patch and then manual confirmation that it equals or is semantically equivalent to the developer patch. The action categories and semantic-relation labels are human analytical judgments, not rewards emitted by the environment.

This contract can observe tool feedback, terminal test outcomes, and human-coded coherence between adjacent components. It cannot establish whether a thought is faithful to hidden model computation, whether a passing patch is generally correct beyond the benchmark tests, or whether a relation label has objective ground truth. The paper reports collaborative discussion and partial agreement checks, but not a numeric agreement statistic or a complete double-coding protocol.

Relative to the original RepairAgent, AutoCodeRover, and OpenHands evaluations, the change is to retain and compare internal trajectories instead of only terminal success. Relative to interpretability centered on a single prediction, it studies behavior across time; relative to work that learns from past trajectories, it supplies an empirical audit methodology rather than an optimizer. The direction signal is the combination of successful and failed episodes, a shared action vocabulary, and explicit feedback-use labels—not the introduction of new tasks or new agents.
