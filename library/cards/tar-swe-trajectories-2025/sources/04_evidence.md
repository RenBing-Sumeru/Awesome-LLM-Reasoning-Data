The empirical base is 120 trajectories containing 2,822 LLM interactions, evenly divided across RepairAgent, AutoCodeRover V1, and OpenHands CodeAct. The study intentionally includes success and failure: the official release lists 10 resolved and 30 unresolved instances per agent. This scale supports descriptive comparison of the sampled logs, not population-level claims about all software-engineering agents.

For RQ1, the paper reports mean trajectory lengths of 34 iterations for RepairAgent, 29 for OpenHands, and 6 for AutoCodeRover. Approximate mean total token counts are 220K, 1.2M, and 23K in the same agent order. These figures are conditioned on different agent architectures, task substrates, model identities, and iteration budgets; they do not isolate an architecture or model effect.

For RQ2, successful trajectories more often combine exploration, explanation, fix generation, and testing. Failed trajectories show repeated non-adaptive cycles and validation anti-patterns, including producing a fix without testing it. The authors derive these observations from the eight-way action coding and action 4-grams after excluding the 8.3% of actions that fit no category. The association is useful for diagnosis but does not establish that a motif causes success or failure.

For RQ3, the authors report that semantic misalignment is rare overall but appears more often in some failed trajectories; agent- and outcome-specific percentages are presented in Table II. The five relation families expose whether a thought motivates an action, whether successive thoughts/actions progress coherently, and whether a result is acknowledged or changes the next behavior. These are human-coded interpretations. No complete numeric reliability study is reported, so fine-grained label differences should not be treated as calibrated ground truth.

The official repository provides evidence that the analytical artifacts exist: per-agent parsed views, action-category CSVs, five annotation directories, RQ1 metrics, parsers, and successful/failed instance lists. A representative OpenHands record preserves a natural-language thought, tool name, and serialized arguments. This verifies the release shape, not immutable replay completeness.

The results are behavioral descriptions and design hypotheses. They do not demonstrate that the release is high-quality training data, that one agent is intrinsically better, or that the annotated patterns improve a trained model. Benchmark performance is used to label terminal outcomes; it is not proof of annotation reliability, release completeness, decontamination, or training value.
