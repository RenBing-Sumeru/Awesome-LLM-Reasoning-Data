The prior baseline is terminal evaluation of individual software-repair agents: a benchmark records whether a generated patch passes tests or is accepted as correct, but that scalar outcome hides the sequence of reasoning, tool calls, observations, retries, and ignored feedback. Agent-log viewers expose interactions, and trajectory-learning methods can optimize from past runs, but neither alone supplies a common empirical coding scheme for comparing three heterogeneous software-engineering agents.

This paper changes the object of study from final patch outcome to the ordered thought-action-result episode. It normalizes RepairAgent, AutoCodeRover V1, and OpenHands CodeAct logs; introduces a shared eight-class action vocabulary; compares fixed action 4-grams; and open-codes five types of adjacency relations across roughly 14,000 component pairs. Keeping about 30 failures and 10 successes per agent makes failed adaptation and ignored feedback first-class analysis objects.

The feedback interface is also made explicit. Environment observations are separated from terminal success, and both are separated from human judgments about action type and semantic coherence. That distinction is useful for reasoning-data research because it prevents a test-passing label from being silently treated as step supervision and prevents human relation labels from being described as environment rewards.

What is not new includes the three agents, Defects4J and SWE-bench Lite tasks, their original terminal evaluators, tool-use logs, and general qualitative coding. The work does not introduce a new policy, replayable environment, benchmark split, reward model, or demonstrated training recipe. Its concrete novelty is the cross-agent empirical representation and annotation methodology applied to both successful and failed trajectories.

The direction signal is auditability below terminal reward: future releases can preserve state/action/observation fields, mark feedback uptake, and diagnose repeated unproductive loops. Before reuse, however, the community must verify that the published parser outputs match raw logs, the episode manifests match every analysis table, relation labels are reliable, and the release can be pinned and replayed. Scale and an award do not substitute for those checks.
