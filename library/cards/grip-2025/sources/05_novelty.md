The baseline construction interface is a seed question or an externally supplied topic. Rewriting preserves the seed's wording and concept bundle; topic-conditioned synthesis can still depend on proprietary models or a manually curated topic source. GRIP instead makes a derived graph the generation substrate: explicit edges preserve observed co-occurrence, while two-hop and hub-centered three-hop paths deliberately expose concept pairs that did not co-occur in one seed problem. Community cliques add 3- or 4-concept conditions. (Paper Figure 1 and Sections 2.2-3.3.)

The direction signal is operational rather than rhetorical. Relationship type, graph distance, hub status, predicted difficulty, solution-teacher size, problem threshold, and unanimous solution voting become separately testable construction variables. The hop ablation shows why this matters: moving farther in the graph increases combinatorial supply while lowering average judge score, creating an explicit diversity-quality-cost tradeoff.

GRIP also replaces a single closed-source judge with three open mathematical models. That improves inspectability of model identities and the acceptance rule, but it does not create a new correctness oracle. The weights remain unknown, the models can share errors, and binary solution votes judge whole text rather than steps. The official 24,957-row sample is a useful release improvement over paper-only recipes, yet its four-field schema omits the variables required to test the claimed graph mechanism row by row.

Concept graphs, LLM synthesis, difficulty routing, ensemble judgment, and supervised fine-tuning are not individually novel. The concrete contribution is their composition around implicit graph relationships and a large expansion study. Before reuse, the key quality signal should be reproduced with released branch labels, rejection logs, independent verification, and controlled comparisons that hold generator and sample budget fixed.
