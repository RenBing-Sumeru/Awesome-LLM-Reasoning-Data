BEARCUBS combines three choices that matter for agent-data auditing: tasks are grounded in the changing live web rather than a frozen simulator; nearly half of the retained questions require multimodal or interactive evidence; and each accepted item is backed internally by a short gold answer plus a viable human path and visited sites. This makes the benchmark useful for asking where an agent failed—perception, interface control, navigation, planning, source selection, or answer synthesis—rather than reporting only a static QA score.

Its construction also makes search resistance an explicit selection criterion. Ordinary Google Search should not reveal the answer directly, and proposed multimodal items are tested with OpenAI Deep Research to remove text-only workarounds. The removal of 13 candidates is concrete evidence that negative selection shaped the task surface. However, the rejected items, decisions, and record-level filter outcomes are not released, so the construction audit remains aggregate.

The feedback design is deliberately narrow: one concise answer is judged for direct entailment of a gold answer, while trajectories and source attribution support post-hoc analysis. This separation is analytically useful because it reveals that an answer can be correct even when its source is secondary or ungrounded. It also exposes a limitation: BEARCUBS does not provide a step-level verifier, path-validity reward, or environmental success predicate.

The work does not introduce an agent-training objective, teacher-generated rollout corpus, preference dataset, reward model, deterministic browser environment, or replay system. Nor does the question archive publish the internal paths that motivate its trajectory analysis. Its contribution is therefore benchmark construction and failure-oriented evaluation, not an open trajectory-data release.

For this atlas, the release gap is itself a substantive audit lesson. “Trajectories were collected and analyzed” and “trajectories are public with lineage, failure retention, rights, versioning, and replay” are different claims. BEARCUBS supports the first and does not establish the second.
