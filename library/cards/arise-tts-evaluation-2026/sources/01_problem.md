Test-time scaling is often summarized by an accuracy–token curve or a slope computed from aggregate points. Such summaries can hide what happened to individual problems. A model may solve some formerly incorrect samples with extra thought while also converting previously correct samples into errors; an aggregate slope may even reward a run that used more tokens for worse results. Stochastic decoding further makes both token use and accuracy fluctuate, complicating fair comparison across models.

The paper asks how to evaluate the effectiveness of additional inference computation without treating all performance changes as equally good. It seeks a metric that follows individual samples across successive compute levels, penalizes negative scaling rather than averaging it away, and spends evaluation trials where the observed result is uncertain. The goal is a reliable quality-cost comparison, not another strategy for generating answers.
