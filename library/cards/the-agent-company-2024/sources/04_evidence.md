The benchmark evidence is anchored to version 1.0.0 and 175 tasks. The archival appendix reports 69 software-engineering, 28 project-management, 14 data-science, 15 administrative, 29 human-resources, 12 finance, and 8 other tasks; these counts sum to 175. The construction evidence is author-reported: 20 contributors worked for two months, about 3,000 person-hours, and some tasks took more than ten hours. The authors also report evaluator tests, screenshot evidence, code review, manual double-checks, and independent checkpoint-importance review. These procedures document a construction effort, but the absence of released per-task review logs prevents a record-level quality audit.

For evaluator composition, 51 tasks (29%) involve LLM evaluation; the remainder are primarily deterministic state/trajectory checks. Paper baselines use Claude-3-5-Sonnet-20241022 for LLM evaluators and simulated colleagues. The archival paper says assessors were reviewed and tested by 3–5 contributors plus CI. This supports the existence of a mixed verification contract, not an estimate of judge error or inter-run variance, because no independent judge-calibration corpus is released.

The key performance numbers must be versioned rather than read as one improvement curve. The official 2024-12-17 run with OpenHands 0.14.2 and Claude-3.5-Sonnet reports 42/175 full successes (24.0%), a 34.4% partial score, 29.17 average steps, and $6.34 average cost per task. The archival NeurIPS paper reports Gemini 2.5 Pro with OpenHands 0.28.1 at 30.3% full success and 39.3% partial score, with 27.2 average steps and $4.2 average cost per task. The harness version, model, and paper version changed; therefore 24.0% to 30.3% is baseline/version drift, not a controlled ablation and not evidence that the dataset itself improved.

The release evidence is stronger than a paper-only benchmark: the inspected official baseline directory contains results and trajectories for successful, partial, and zero-score tasks, plus per-step screenshots. The public submission contract asks for the same artifact classes. This supports failure analysis for the inspected run, but it does not prove that every historical third-party run is complete or consistently licensed.

The paper's error analysis includes premature completion in social interaction, browsing/UI failure, and shortcuts that satisfy an evaluator-visible state without satisfying the intended task, such as renaming a user. These are observed baseline failures and concrete warnings about environment/evaluator brittleness; they are not exhaustive failure rates. Human feasibility and evaluator checks were performed, but no human performance baseline was collected because tasks can take from roughly ten minutes to several hours. The authors also caution that the task set is not exhaustive or representative enough for broad job-automation claims. None of these benchmark results demonstrates training-data quality or benefit from training on the released logs.
